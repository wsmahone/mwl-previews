Hormone therapy for menopause: why this practice uses pellets

Pellets are the delivery method Medical Weight Loss NY prefers for hormone therapy. The reasons are practical:

  • Placed a few times a year, so there is no hormone cream or gel to apply each day
  • Released over months rather than applied every day
  • Dosed for you from your labs, your symptoms, and your weight, then rechecked with bloodwork, typically 6 to 8 weeks after placement
  • Placed under the skin, not taken by mouth
  • Plant-based and made from organic yams; bioidentical means identical to what your body makes, which makes it different from other (synthetic) forms

Depending on your labs and symptoms, your plan may include bioidentical testosterone, estrogen, or both, where appropriate. If you have a uterus and take estrogen, your provider also addresses protecting the uterine lining with progesterone.

Not the right fit for everyone

Some women do better with a different form of hormone therapy, some need additional evaluation first, and some should not use hormone therapy at all, including most women with a history of breast cancer, blood clots, stroke, or heart disease. Your provider reviews your personal and family history and tells you plainly. If hormones are not the answer, the weight, sleep, and nutrition side of your plan still moves forward.

How often are pellets placed, and how long do they last for women?

Women at our practice have typically returned for a new pellet placement about every three to five months. The interval is individualized.

Your provider sets your schedule from your labs and your symptoms, not a calendar, and tells you when bloodwork is needed before a placement.

What does the pellet procedure involve?

A brief in-office procedure: after numbing the area with a local anesthetic, your provider makes a tiny incision, usually just above the buttocks, slips small plant-based pellets under the skin, and closes it with a small adhesive strip. No stitches are needed.

The appointment is about 30 minutes, and the placement itself takes only a few minutes. Aftercare is to avoid heavy lifting, repetitive squatting, and exercise for 3 to 4 days; mild swelling at the site can occur. The most common early effects of the hormones themselves are fluid retention, breast tenderness, mood changes, acne, and hair changes, including new facial or body hair, usually in the first few weeks and usually temporary. Less common risks depend on which hormones you use and on your own history, which your provider reviews before recommending anything.

How long until I feel a difference?

It varies. In Dr. Scinta's experience, many patients notice a change within the first few weeks, and your provider formally assesses your response with labs at the recheck, typically 6 to 8 weeks after placement.

Some women need a dose adjustment or a booster at the recheck before they feel their best. The Menopause Society calls hormone therapy the most effective treatment for hot flashes and night sweats; the effect on energy, sleep, mood, and weight depends on what else is going on for you. Results vary from person to person.

What about the breast cancer and blood clot risks I have read about?

Both depend on the type of hormone therapy and on your own history, and both are smaller and more specific than the headlines suggested.

Estrogen taken as a pill is linked to a higher clot risk because it passes through the liver first; estrogen given through the skin does not, and observational studies suggest a lower clot risk with patches and gels than with pills. Pellets are also not taken by mouth, but those studies did not include pellets, so there is less data on them. For breast cancer, the large Women's Health Initiative trial linked estrogen plus a synthetic progestin to a small increase after several years of use, while estrogen alone was not linked to an increase. The Menopause Society's 2022 position statement supports hormone therapy for healthy women with symptoms who are under 60 or within 10 years of menopause. That statement is about hormone therapy in general, not about any one delivery method. Your provider reviews your personal and family history before recommending anything, and lower risk is never zero risk.

Who should not use hormone therapy?

Women with a history of breast cancer or another hormone-sensitive cancer, a history of blood clots, a clotting disorder, stroke, or heart disease, unexplained vaginal bleeding, or active liver disease generally should not use hormone therapy, or need a different plan and additional evaluation first.

Starting more than 10 years after menopause, or after age 60, also calls for a more careful look at risks and benefits. Your provider decides based on your history, examination, and labs, and will tell you plainly if hormone therapy is not appropriate for you. If it is not, the weight, sleep, and nutrition side of your plan can still move forward.
