Perimenopause and menopause symptoms women get evaluated for

Perimenopause and menopause are worked out from your history, your symptoms, and lab work, not from a checklist on a website. But women usually decide to get evaluated after noticing changes like these, especially when several show up together and do not let up:

  • Unexplained weight gain, or weight that will not come off, especially around the middle
  • Hot flashes and night sweats
  • Poor sleep or insomnia
  • Mood swings, anxiety, or irritability
  • Brain fog or difficulty concentrating
  • Fatigue and low energy
  • Low libido
  • Decreased muscle tone

These changes have more than one possible cause, and hormones are only part of the picture. The evaluation exists to find out what is going on for you, including when the answer is not hormones.

Menopause weight gain, treated in the same office

Weight gain during perimenopause and menopause is not a willpower problem. Falling estrogen shifts where the body stores fat, toward the abdomen. Testosterone declines gradually with age, and less lean muscle means a slower metabolism. Broken sleep and stress hormones add to it. Here the hormone side and the weight side are evaluated by one team, in one chart, so nobody has to guess which one is holding you back.

What changes at menopause

  • Less estrogen, so fat is stored more around the waist and less on the hips and thighs
  • Lower testosterone with age, so lean muscle drops and metabolism slows with it
  • Poor sleep and higher stress hormones, which drive cravings and fat storage around the middle
  • A thicker waist can mean more visceral fat, the type linked to heart disease risk

Dr. Scinta walks through each cause in What causes an increase in belly fat after menopause?

Hormone therapy for menopause: why this practice uses pellets

Pellets are the delivery method Medical Weight Loss NY prefers for hormone therapy. The reasons are practical:

  • Placed a few times a year, so there is no hormone cream or gel to apply each day
  • Released over months rather than applied every day
  • Dosed for you from your labs, your symptoms, and your weight, then rechecked with bloodwork
  • Placed under the skin, not taken by mouth. The blood clot question is answered in the FAQ.
  • Bioidentical means identical to what the body makes, which makes it different from other (synthetic) forms; the pellets are plant-based and made from organic yams

Which hormones? Depending on your labs and symptoms, your plan may include bioidentical testosterone, estrogen, or both, where appropriate. If you have a uterus and take estrogen, your provider also addresses protecting the uterine lining with progesterone.

Pellets are not the right option for every woman

Some women do better with a different form or route of hormone therapy, some need additional evaluation first, and some should not use hormone therapy at all. Your provider reviews your personal and family history, tells you what monitoring is required, and says plainly if another approach fits you better. If hormones are not the answer, the weight, sleep, and nutrition side of your plan still moves forward.

Questions women ask before booking

Is it perimenopause or menopause, and how do I know which one I am in?

Menopause is the point when you have gone 12 months in a row without a period; the average age in the United States is 51. Perimenopause is the transition before it, often starting in the 40s, when cycles change and symptoms begin.

Hot flashes, sleep problems, mood changes, and weight gain can start well before periods stop, and hormone levels swing during this stage, so a single lab value does not tell the whole story. Your provider uses your history, your symptoms, and lab work together to determine where you are, which shapes what treatment, if any, makes sense.

How do I know if hormones are causing my symptoms, or if it is something else?

You do not know until you are evaluated. Symptoms are a reason to get checked, not a diagnosis.

Fatigue, brain fog, and weight gain have other possible causes, including thyroid problems, sleep disorders, and medication effects. The evaluation includes a comprehensive physical exam, a full history, and lab work, so your provider can tell you whether hormones are part of the picture and what else might explain how you feel.

What kind of hormone therapy do you use for menopause symptoms?

At Medical Weight Loss NY, hormone therapy for women in perimenopause and menopause is bioidentical hormone therapy, delivered mainly as pellets placed under the skin.

Depending on your labs and symptoms, your plan may include bioidentical testosterone, estrogen, or both, where appropriate. Whether it is appropriate for you is decided after your evaluation, not before.

What are hormone pellets, and how are they placed?

Hormone pellets are small and plant-based, made from organic yams, about the size of a grain of rice, and placed under the skin in a brief in-office procedure.

After numbing the area with a local anesthetic, your provider makes a tiny incision, usually just above the buttocks, slips the pellets under the skin, and closes it with a small adhesive strip; no stitches are needed. The appointment is about 30 minutes, and the placement itself takes only a few minutes. Aftercare is to avoid heavy lifting, repetitive squatting, and exercise for 3 to 4 days; mild swelling at the site can occur.

How long until I feel a difference?

It varies. In Dr. Scinta's experience, many patients notice a change within the first few weeks, and your provider formally assesses your response with labs at the recheck, typically 6 to 8 weeks after placement.

Everyone responds differently, and some women need a dose adjustment or a booster at the recheck before they feel their best. The Menopause Society calls hormone therapy the most effective treatment for hot flashes and night sweats; the effect on energy, sleep, mood, and weight depends on what else is going on for you. Results vary from person to person.

How often are pellets placed, and how long do they last for women?

Women at our practice have typically returned for a new pellet placement about every three to five months. The interval is individualized.

Your provider sets your schedule from your labs and your symptoms, not a calendar, and tells you when bloodwork is needed before a placement.

What are the side effects of hormone pellets?

The most common effects show up in the first few weeks and are usually temporary: fluid retention, swelling of the hands and feet, breast tenderness, mood swings or irritability, acne, and hair changes, including new facial or body hair or thinning scalp hair.

Placement itself can cause mild swelling or bruising at the site. Less common risks depend on which hormones you use and on your own history, which is why your provider reviews your personal and family history before recommending anything and monitors your labs after you start. Call the office with anything that concerns you.

Does hormone therapy increase the risk of breast cancer?

It depends on the type of hormone therapy, how long you use it, and your own history, and the risk is smaller and more specific than the headlines from 2002 suggested.

In the large Women's Health Initiative trial, estrogen combined with a synthetic progestin was linked to a small increase in breast cancer after several years of use, while estrogen alone was not. The Menopause Society's 2022 position statement reviews this evidence and supports hormone therapy for healthy women with symptoms who are under 60 or within 10 years of menopause. That statement is about hormone therapy in general, not about any one delivery method. Dr. Scinta has written about common hormone therapy myths and questions. Your provider reviews your personal and family history, including any history of breast cancer, before recommending anything, and breast screening stays part of your care.

Does hormone therapy cause blood clots?

Estrogen taken as a pill is associated with a higher risk of blood clots because it passes through the liver first. Estrogen given through the skin does not, and observational studies suggest a lower clot risk with patches and gels than with pills. Pellets are also placed under the skin rather than taken by mouth, but those studies compared pills with patches and gels, not pellets, so there is less data on pellets.

Lower risk is not zero risk. If you have had a blood clot, a stroke, or a clotting disorder, or you have other risk factors, your provider reviews that history before any hormone decision and may tell you hormone therapy is not appropriate for you.

Who should not use hormone therapy?

Women with a history of breast cancer or another hormone-sensitive cancer, a history of blood clots, a clotting disorder, stroke, or heart disease, unexplained vaginal bleeding, or active liver disease generally should not use hormone therapy, or need a different plan and additional evaluation first.

Starting more than 10 years after menopause, or after age 60, also calls for a more careful look at risks and benefits. Your provider decides based on your history, examination, and labs, and will tell you plainly if hormone therapy is not appropriate for you. If it is not, the weight, sleep, and nutrition side of your plan can still move forward.

Why do women gain weight, especially belly fat, during menopause?

Falling estrogen changes where fat is stored, sending more of it to the abdomen, and the gradual drop in testosterone with age reduces lean muscle, which slows metabolism. You can gain inches at the waist without gaining much on the scale.

Sleep loss and stress raise cortisol and appetite hormones, which add to cravings and fat storage around the middle, and most people also move less in midlife. A thicker waist often means more visceral fat, the type tied to heart disease risk. Dr. Scinta covers each of these causes in What causes an increase in belly fat after menopause?

Will hormone therapy help me lose weight?

Hormone therapy is not a weight loss treatment, and we do not prescribe it as one.

What it can do, when it is appropriate for you, is treat the hot flashes, night sweats, and broken sleep that make the weight work harder to keep up, and some studies suggest it may limit the shift of fat toward the abdomen during the transition, though the effect is small. The weight itself is treated in our medical weight loss program, with a registered dietitian, body composition tracking, and prescription weight loss medication when appropriate. Both can be planned at the same evaluation.
