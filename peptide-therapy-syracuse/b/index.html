Are peptides FDA-approved?

Most are not. A few peptides are FDA-approved prescription drugs, including insulin and the GLP-1 medications semaglutide and tirzepatide. The peptides used in recovery, skin, and energy protocols are usually compounded, and compounded drugs are not FDA-approved. The FDA does not check their safety or effectiveness before they are sold.

The FDA's position is moving. In 2023 it flagged many of the peptides marketed for recovery and healthy aging as compounding ingredients that may present significant safety risks, citing possible immune reactions and limited human safety data. It has since moved several of them to a nominated-but-withdrawn list after their nominations were withdrawn, and on July 23 and 24, 2026 its Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee met to consider whether seven of them should be added to the list of substances pharmacies may compound. Advisory committee recommendations are not binding on the FDA, and those are steps in a review, not approval. As of September 2026, none of them is an FDA-approved drug. Sources: FDA, bulk drug substances that may present significant safety risks and FDA, Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee meeting, July 23 to 24, 2026.

We do not describe any protocol as FDA-approved. At your consultation your provider tells you the status of each peptide in your protocol, and what is and is not known about it, before you decide. Results vary.

What is peptide therapy?

Peptide therapy is the use of specific peptides, short chains of amino acids that act as signaling molecules in the body, in a set protocol aimed at supporting a process such as tissue repair, recovery, collagen production, or energy metabolism.

At Medical Weight Loss NY it is delivered as a physician-guided program: a 45-minute medical consultation, a protocol chosen from five published programs, a phone consultation at two weeks, and a reevaluation at four weeks. It is not a cure for any condition, and results vary.

How are peptides taken?

It depends on the peptide. In this field, peptides are commonly given as small self-administered injections under the skin, and some come as capsules, nasal sprays, or creams. The form your protocol uses is explained and taught at your consultation.

If needles are a concern, say so at the visit. Your provider will tell you what your options are for the goal you have.

How long does a peptide protocol run?

Protocol length is set by your provider and revisited at each checkpoint. Pricing is monthly, with a phone consultation at two weeks and a reevaluation at four weeks that decides whether to continue, adjust, or stop.

The practice does not publish a fixed program length. How long you continue depends on your goal and your response, and your provider will give you a realistic expectation at the consultation. After the four-week reevaluation, follow-up continues on the schedule your provider sets.

What are the side effects of peptide therapy?

Side effects depend on the peptide. Commonly reported ones include irritation or redness at an injection site, headache, flushing, nausea, fatigue, and water retention. Some peptides can affect blood sugar or appetite, and any injected peptide carries a risk of an immune reaction.

Because most of these peptides have limited long-term human safety data, unknown risks are part of the honest picture. That is one reason the two-week and four-week checkpoints exist. Report anything unexpected to the office rather than waiting for your next visit.

Is peptide therapy safe?

No peptide protocol is risk-free, and the practice does not describe any protocol as safe in absolute terms. Most peptides used in recovery, skin, and energy protocols are compounded, are not FDA-approved, and have limited long-term human safety data, so unknown risks are part of the honest picture.

That is why every protocol here starts with a medical consultation with a provider and is checked at two weeks and four weeks, and why anything unexpected should be reported to the office rather than waiting for the next visit. Commonly reported side effects are covered in the side-effects question in this FAQ.

How long does it take for peptide therapy to work?

The practice does not publish a timeline, because response differs by person, protocol, and goal, and most peptides used in this field have limited long-term research behind them.

Your response is reviewed at the two-week phone consultation and the four-week reevaluation, and your provider gives you a realistic expectation for your protocol at the consultation. Results vary.

Who should not use peptide therapy?

Anyone who is pregnant or breastfeeding, anyone with an active cancer or a history of cancer, and anyone hoping to treat a diagnosed disease with peptides instead of standard care should not start a protocol without that conversation first. Other health conditions and medications can rule out specific peptides.

Your provider decides based on your history and will tell you plainly if peptide therapy is not appropriate for you.

Does insurance cover peptide therapy?

Generally, no. Peptide therapy is self-pay at our practice, and insurance plans do not typically cover peptide protocols, which are not FDA-approved treatments.

Our practice is out of network and does not bill insurance. Ask about payment options when we call you back.

How is peptide therapy different from GLP-1 weight loss medication?

GLP-1 medications such as semaglutide and tirzepatide are peptides, but they are FDA-approved prescription drugs for weight management and type 2 diabetes, and at our practice they belong to the GLP-1 weight loss program, with its own lab work and evaluation.

The protocols on this page are aimed at recovery, energy, skin, gut, and post-illness fatigue, not weight loss. If weight is your main goal, start with the GLP-1 program instead.
